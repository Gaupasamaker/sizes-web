How to choose a bikini or swimsuit size when shopping online.

Your usual size is a useful start, but swimwear also depends on bust, underbust, hips, torso length and the support built into each design.

Updated 202610 min readSwimwear · Online

A familiar summer scene starts long before you reach the beach. You find a bikini you like, select your usual size and add it to the basket. Then the questions begin. Will the top stay in place? Will the bottoms dig in? Will the one-piece feel too short through the body? You open the size chart and end up moving between two options that both look plausible.

The problem is not that your body is difficult. Swimwear packs several decisions into very little fabric: support, coverage, stretch, waist height and freedom of movement. The same size may work in adjustable bikini bottoms and fail in a structured swimsuit. The simplest approach is to split the decision into the parts that actually need to fit.

Key idea

Choose swimwear by the area that needs the most precision. The size label points you in a direction; bust, underbust, hips, torso and design make the final decision.

Why your usual size may not be enough

A few extra centimetres in a relaxed T-shirt may simply create a looser fit. In a bikini top, the same difference can change how the band supports, where the cup sits or whether a strap slips. In bikini bottoms, it changes waist pressure and coverage. A one-piece also has to work vertically from shoulder to crotch.

Construction changes how you read the size. A triangle top with adjustable ties can adapt through the band and straps. A bandeau relies more heavily on a stable band. Tie-side bottoms tolerate changes that a fixed high-rise cut cannot. Two pieces with the same label can call for different decisions without either being poorly made.

Start with the garment, not the label

Before measuring, look at how the piece is built. Does the top have underwire, fixed cups, removable pads or just fabric? Are the straps adjustable? Do the bottoms tie at the sides or have a fixed waistband? Is the swimsuit soft, shaping, compressive or particularly low cut? These details show you where adjustment is available and where the size needs to be accurate.

Think about use too. A sunbathing bikini may prioritise adjustability and your preferred coverage. A swimsuit for swimming must stay stable as you move. Swimwear for walking, playing with children or spending a full day at the beach needs reliable support. Size makes more sense when it is connected to the day you will actually have.

The measurements that help

For a bikini top, measure around the fullest part of your bust and directly underneath it. Keep the tape horizontal, close to the body and comfortable. For bottoms, measure your waist and the fullest part of your hips. Do not hold your breath or pull the tape tight: you want a measurement you can repeat, not the smallest number you can produce.

For a one-piece, bust, waist and hips still matter, but torso length can decide the fit. If straps pull down, the neckline shifts or the crotch feels uncomfortable while the width looks right, the vertical length may be the problem. Compare with a swimsuit that already works or follow the brand's torso measurement when one is provided.

A practical example

Your bust sits in one size and your hips in the next. With separates, you can choose a different size for each piece. With a one-piece, prioritise the least adjustable area, then check that the rest stays stable.

Choosing the bikini top

The underband does much of the support work. It should feel firm and stay horizontal instead of riding up your back. If a brand uses bra-style numbers, do not automatically translate them into S, M or L. Compare bust and underbust with that specific chart.

The cup should contain without cutting in or leaving large gaps. A small fold may be part of the design, but an edge that presses or overflow usually points to too little room. At the other extreme, a cup that moves away when you lean forward may be too large or simply the wrong shape. A different model can solve more than a different size.

Adjustable straps refine the fit, but they should not provide all the support. If you must shorten them fully to keep the top in place, check the band. If they dig in even when positioned correctly, the design may not distribute support in the way you need.

Choosing the bikini bottoms

Hip measurement is often the strongest reference, but the cut changes how the garment feels. High-waisted bottoms distribute pressure differently from Brazilian or tie-side styles. Coverage also affects how much fabric you need to feel comfortable sitting, walking and getting out of the water.

Look for a waist that stays in place without pressing sharply or gaping. Leg openings should follow the body rather than rolling or shifting. If you are between sizes and the design ties at the sides, the adjustment may give you room. With a fixed waist and firmer fabric, give more weight to your actual waist and hip measurements.

A one-piece adds another dimension

A swimsuit can fit in width and still be too short through the torso. It can work at the hips but open at the bust. That is why you should test it by lifting your arms, sitting, bending and mimicking a swimming stroke. The neckline and leg should remain where the design intended.

Shaping and compressive styles need contact with the body to work, but contact is not the same as restriction. You should be able to breathe and move. If the fabric causes pain, pulls heavily from the shoulders or creates constant pressure, you are not gaining support; you are losing comfort.

What changes in the water

Swimwear is made to work when wet, but fabrics differ in firmness and recovery. A loose garment may shift, while one that is too tight can limit movement. Read the composition, fit notes and reviews that describe real use rather than only how the piece looks in a photograph.

You do not need to wet a new garment at home to make a sensible decision. Check that it remains stable as you move and does not need constant adjustment. Keep hygiene liners and tags in place during the fitting and always follow the retailer's return conditions.

When you are between two sizes

There is no universal rule to size up or down. With a fixed-band top, prioritise an underband that supports without restricting. With adjustable bottoms, choose the size that gives the coverage you want. With a one-piece, prioritise the hardest area to adjust, often bust, hips or torso length.

State the risk in one sentence. “The smaller size may cut into my hips.” “The larger one may gape at the bust.” “This swimsuit may be too short through the body.” Once the risk is clear, the choice becomes a practical comparison rather than a contest between two labels.

Checklist before ordering

  • Identify whether the band, straps or waist can be adjusted.
  • Measure bust and underbust for the top.
  • Measure waist and hips for the bottoms.
  • Check torso length for one-piece swimsuits.
  • Read whether the fabric is soft, firm, shaping or compressive.
  • Decide the coverage and activity you need.
  • Test movement before removing tags or hygiene liners.

Common mistakes that lead to returns

The first is buying both halves in one size when the retailer sells them separately. The second is choosing from the model photograph without checking cup structure or coverage. The third is assuming smaller always means more support. Support comes from pattern, band and construction, not from tolerating pressure.

Another mistake is saving only “M” or “95” as a reference. That size belongs to a particular design. Add two useful words: “firm bandeau”, “high waist”, “short torso” or “roomy cup”. Those notes stop you repeating the same uncertainty next summer.
