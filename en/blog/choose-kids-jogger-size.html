How to choose a kids' jogger size without slipping or slowing them down.

Waist, hip, rise and length: a practical story for choosing trousers that can handle class, break time and after-school activities.

Updated 202612 min readFamilies · School

The morning starts with a race against the clock. Someone is looking for a sock, the school bag is waiting by the door and the joggers seem to fit perfectly in front of the mirror. Half an hour later, in the playground, the waistband keeps sliding down and the hem catches under a trainer every time the child runs.

The problem is not necessarily a poor garment or a bad choice. Often the decision was made by looking only at age or the number on the label. Children's joggers have to do several jobs at once: stay up without pressure, allow sitting and squatting, leave sensible room to grow and work with the fabric and shoes they will actually wear.

Choosing the right size means turning an everyday situation into a few useful measurements. This article follows that process: find a reference garment, measure it without stretching it, read the product chart and run a short movement test before removing the tags.

Key idea

Age can point you to a chart, but waist, hip, rise and length tell you much more about how joggers will work. Compare a pair that already feels comfortable and leave room for movement, not a whole size of extra fabric everywhere.

The right joggers have to pass a movement test

Joggers do not spend the day standing still. They may be worn while sitting on the floor, running during PE, travelling in a car and doing homework in the same afternoon. A photograph taken standing up cannot tell the whole story. A waistband can look fine and loosen on the stairs; a rise can feel generous until the child has to lift a knee.

Before measuring, think about the main use. Are these trousers for school PE, training, lounging at home or everyday wear with a sweatshirt? A lightweight jersey pair asks for a different feel from brushed fleece. The fit does not need to be identical in every situation, but it should be chosen deliberately.

It also helps to separate two wishes that often get mixed together: making the garment last through the season and making it comfortable today. A little ease can help. Buying a full size up purely to anticipate growth may leave the drawcord too long, put the knees in the wrong place or make the child roll up the legs every morning.

Age is a starting point, not a measurement

Age labels make a collection easier to browse, but they group bodies with very different proportions. Two eight-year-olds may have different height, waist, leg length and hip shape. Even one child may need different sizes in a slim jogger, a straight trouser and a sports style with a cuff because the fabric is distributed differently.

Use age to open the right chart, not to finish the decision without checking anything else. Look for height, waist, hip or inside-leg measurements. If the brand only lists age ranges, use the product photographs and cut description as context, but try to find garment or body measurements in the product information.

Back-to-school shopping adds another trap. It is easy to buy in August while imagining November and forget that the joggers must work in September. Adjustable waists and durable fabric are usually a better way to plan for the term than a complete size jump.

A reference garment tells a better story than intuition

Find trousers the child has worn several times without asking you to pull them up or change them. They do not have to be new. A worn pair can be more revealing: it shows where the waistband sits after sitting down, whether the cuff catches, whether the knee pulls and how the fabric changed after washing.

Choose a reference with a similar construction. Stretchy joggers are not a direct comparison for rigid trousers. A cuffed pair should not be measured and then used as the benchmark for a wide straight leg. Note whether the reference is worn with a long T-shirt tucked in, thermal underwear or a belt that changes the volume.

Lay the garment flat on a table, close the button or tie the drawcord as it is normally worn and smooth the fabric with your palm. Do not pull the legs longer or stretch the elastic to its maximum. Photograph the layout and write down the model name. Your next purchase will then have a more useful memory than “the size they wore last year”.

What to measure on kids' joggers

Waist: support without pressure

Measure the waistband from side to side while the garment is flat and double the number to estimate the circumference. If it has elastic, record the relaxed measurement and how far it can expand without forcing it. With a drawcord, check how much adjustment is available and whether it sits centrally. A chart may show body measurements, finished garment measurements or a range; do not treat them as the same thing.

The waistband should hold the trousers while the child walks and sits, without leaving deep marks or needing to be loosened after lunch. A drawcord can correct a small amount of ease, not turn a clearly oversized pair into the right size. If it has to be pulled into a bulky knot, reconsider the size or the model.

Hip and thigh: room to sit down

Measure the widest part of the garment, normally around the hip and top of the thigh. This matters even when the waist is adjustable. Sitting, lifting a knee or putting something in a pocket requires the fabric to redistribute without pulling at the seams. Cargo pockets and panels can change how a garment feels, so note them too.

A tight hip often becomes obvious before the waist looks small. Look for horizontal drag lines, pockets that gape or seams that press when the child squats. Sizing up may help, but first check whether another cut offers the same length with a little more volume.

Rise: the link between comfort and movement

The rise runs from the front waist, through the crotch, to the back waist. Measuring it on a flat garment means placing the centre seam without folding it. There is no need to treat one number as a verdict: compare it with what happens when the child sits. A short rise can pull the waist down, while a very deep rise can bunch up and move the knee position.

For school, the rise needs to allow sitting on a chair or floor, crossing legs and bending to pick up a bag without the back waistband dropping. For sport, watch whether it follows a wide stride. Stretch helps, but it cannot replace an appropriate rise.

Outside and inside length: where the hem lands

Measure outside length from the waist to the hem and, when available, inside leg from crotch to hem. The inside leg is often easier to compare between models, although a cuff changes the way it reads. Check whether the measurement includes the ribbed cuff and whether the waistband was measured flat.

The right length also depends on the shoe. Joggers for trainers may finish close to the ankle; a pair worn with boots or at home may fall differently. A large fold at the ankle is not a permanent solution: it can catch when running and wear the fabric.

Elastic, drawcords and adjusters

A fully elastic waistband is easy for a child to manage, but comfort depends on the elastic recovering its shape. Elastic that is too firm can press during a long school day; tired elastic lets the trousers slide. Read the reviews for comments about the waistband after several washes, not just how it feels on arrival.

A drawcord can refine the waist, although it should not hang too long or loosen while running. For younger children, a simple, well-finished closure may be more practical than a system that needs several knots. Internal adjusters are useful when waist and height are not growing at the same rate: they keep the garment in place without adding a complete size.

An adjustable waist is not a reason to ignore rise or hip room. If the trousers are comfortable only when the waist is pulled very tight, the pattern is not matching the body well. The goal is for the child to walk, sit and play without thinking about the garment.

Fabric changes how a size feels

Cotton with elastane can give and recover. Brushed fleece adds warmth and volume, but may feel less flexible. Lightweight sports fabric may be cut for a larger range of movement. Two trousers with the same labelled length can feel completely different if one stretches and the other holds a more rigid line.

Read the composition and care instructions. The first wash can change the hand and measurements, especially if the garment is dried with heat. Do not add a full size on the assumption that it will shrink: check the label and compare another pair from the same brand if you have one. Record before-and-after measurements when you expect to buy the style again.

Dark colours can hide strain. It may be hard to see whether seams are under pressure or the fabric is pooling behind the knee. Ask the child to move and use your hand to check waist, hip and thigh. The person wearing the garment can tell you things no catalogue image can.

A real example of choosing between two sizes

Imagine Maya is starting an after-school skating class and needs joggers for the journey and warm-up. Her reference pair has a comfortable waist, a rise that does not pull when she squats and a hem that stops just above the trainer. On the new chart, the smaller size matches the waist but is two centimetres smaller at the hip and three centimetres shorter in length.

The larger size gives her room but adds seven centimetres to the leg. Before choosing it automatically, Maya tries the internal adjuster. If it keeps the waist secure without pressure and the cuff controls the extra length without a large fold, it may work. If the rise becomes too low or the hem sits on the sole, another cut with better proportions is the more useful answer.

The decision does not come from an automatic formula. It comes from identifying the measurement that matters for the activity, checking what adjustment the garment provides and seeing how it behaves in motion. Write down the reason. Next time, you will know that hip and inside leg matter more for Maya than an age-based equivalent.

School, sport and home call for different priorities

For school, prioritise a stable waist, easy dressing and a hem that does not drag. There may need to be room for a long T-shirt or for sitting on the floor, but the trousers should not make stairs awkward. If they are part of a uniform, compare the chart with another school pair, not with pyjamas.

For after-school activities, watch lateral movement and fabric recovery. Running, jumping and stretching require an unrestricted crotch and a hem that stays clear. A cuff may help for a fast-moving activity; for football or dance, the best choice also depends on the shoe and the rules.

At home, softness, flexible waist and freedom to sit on the floor may matter most. That still does not mean any oversized pair will do. Trousers that slide down or bunch around the ankle are distracting for hours. Choose the ease the activity needs, not an indiscriminate amount of fabric.

The five-minute movement test

Before removing the tags, try the joggers with the usual underwear and shoes. Ask the child to walk, sit on the floor, squat to pick something up, lift one knee, climb a step and make two gentle jumps. This is not a workout. Five minutes simply reveal whether the garment follows the body or becomes another thing to manage.

Watch whether the waistband moves, the drawcord presses, the rise pulls at the crotch or the hem slips under the sole. Ask where pressure appears, and when: while standing, sitting or running. A specific answer helps separate an incorrect size from a pattern that does not suit the body.

If the child carries a backpack, repeat the test while wearing it. The bag can press the back waistband and alter posture. If the joggers only work while standing still and carrying nothing, they have not passed the test for the day you bought them for.

Shopping online without losing the reference

Save a screenshot or note the product chart with the purchase date. Collections change and brands can update measurements. Keep the cut name, composition, waistband type and the garment measurements you compared. If returns are allowed, check the window before washing or removing tags.

When a chart shows only age and height, lower your confidence. Look for model-specific measurements, ask customer service or compare with trousers from the same brand. Reviews are most useful when they describe waist, length and fit after wear, rather than simply saying the item is nice.

Checklist before choosing a kids' trouser size

The SIZES quick check

  • Decide whether the trousers are for school, sport, home or several uses.
  • Choose a reference garment with a similar cut and fabric.
  • Measure relaxed waist, hip, rise and inside or outside leg.
  • Check whether the chart describes the body or finished garment.
  • Review elastic, drawcord, adjusters, cuffs and pockets.
  • Read composition and care instructions before adding extra room.
  • Test walking, sitting, squatting, lifting a knee and jumping.
  • Repeat the test with the usual bag and shoes.
  • Save the measurement, model, size and real fit notes in SIZES.

Turn a pair that works into a useful reference

The best size is not the one that works once; it is the one that leaves a lesson for the next purchase. After the joggers have survived several school days and a wash, look again. Is the waistband still stable? Does the length keep its proportion? Did the fabric stretch or shrink? Those answers are more valuable than a guess about growth.

Keep a simple note: “kids' joggers, size 10, adjustable waist, correct length with trainers, comfortable sitting, medium fleece”. In a few months, you can tell which part of the reference has changed and which part still applies. The next decision starts with context.

The conclusion

Choosing a kids' jogger size means looking beyond age and the label. Measure a real garment, check waist, hip, rise and length, interpret the fabric and let movement have the final say. Sensible ease allows growth; an oversized garment with poor proportions simply moves the problem somewhere else.

School and after-school clothing should do its job quietly. When the joggers stay in place, allow movement and remain comfortable at the end of the day, buying them stops being a guess. That is the reference worth saving.

Frequently asked questions

How do I know which jogger size my child needs?

Measure the waist, hip, rise and length on trousers that already fit well, then compare them with the product chart. Check the fit sitting, walking and wearing the usual shoes.

Should I buy a larger size so the joggers last?

Not automatically. Some ease can help, but a larger size may add too much length, let the trousers slide down or make movement harder. Check the waist, rise and cut first.

What should I do if my child is between sizes?

Identify which measurement limits the smaller option. If the waist works but the length is excessive, try an adjustable model or another cut before sizing up. If the hip or rise is tight, the larger option may be more comfortable.

How much can joggers shrink?

It depends on the composition, finish and washing method. Read the care label, avoid assuming a fixed percentage and compare the garment after its first wash with the original measurement.
