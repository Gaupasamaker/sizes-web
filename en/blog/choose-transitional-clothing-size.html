How to choose your size for transitional clothing without overbuying.

When a T-shirt, shirt and jacket need to work together, sizing up is not always the answer. The cut, shoulders, fabric and real use tell you how much room you need.

Updated 202611 min readTransitional · Layers

There is a point in the year when the wardrobe seems to lose its instructions. The morning is cool, midday does not need a jacket and by evening another layer feels useful. You order an overshirt to wear over a T-shirt, then discover that the shoulders are right but the back pulls when you cross your arms.

The obvious reaction is to order the next size. Sometimes that works. Often it turns a clean garment into one with a low armhole, long sleeves and a collar that has moved away from the neck. Layering is not about adding unmeasured volume. It is about deciding what goes underneath, what movement each piece must allow and which point cannot fail.

Key idea

Choose with the layer underneath in mind and prioritise the least adjustable area: shoulders and bust in a shirt, waist and hips in trousers, or armhole and torso length in a jacket.

Transitional clothing does not automatically mean one size up

A layering garment needs functional ease, not necessarily a larger label. That distinction matters. Functional ease lets you breathe, sit, raise your arms and wear a first layer without distorting the pattern. Accidental volume makes everything bigger at once: shoulders, sleeves, length and circumference.

Define the use before looking at the label. A T-shirt worn alone can sit closer to the body. A shirt buttoned over a T-shirt needs more room through the bust and back. A light jacket may need more arm movement, but it should not drop far below the shoulder because of that.

Start with a layer that already works

Find a T-shirt, shirt or jacket you wear often and can move in naturally. It does not have to be identical, but it should have a similar structure. Lay it flat and measure the bust, shoulders, length and sleeve. For trousers, add waist, hips, rise and inseam.

Garment measurements tell a different story from body measurements. A body chart helps you enter a brand's range; a garment measurement shows where the pattern has placed the space. If you have both, use them together. If you only have one, read the fit description and look at front and back images.

T-shirts: the first layer sets the starting point

A cotton jersey T-shirt with some stretch can tolerate a closer fit. If it will sit under a shirt or overshirt, make sure the shoulder seams are not already at their limit. An oversized first layer creates folds under the second and can make you think you need more size than you do.

Check the neck, armhole and length. The neck should not press or open when you move your head. The armhole should let you raise your arm without pulling the whole garment upward. The hem can be longer if it is tucked into another layer, but it should not create a lump that changes the outer layer's fall.

Shirts and overshirts: shoulders before volume

The shoulder seam is especially useful. In a regular cut it usually sits near the end of your natural shoulder; in a relaxed cut it may fall further, but it should look intentional. If you size up to gain room across the bust, check that the sleeve does not become too long and the collar does not shift towards the back.

Button the garment over the T-shirt you would normally wear and cross your arms. Buttons should stay flat, the back should follow movement and the armhole should not cut in. If you wear it open, test that position too: an open overshirt needs drape, not a structure that keeps sliding off.

The same M can solve different problems

An M can work in a regular shirt and feel short across the back in a stiff overshirt. The opposite can also happen: a relaxed overshirt may have enough room in M, while L only adds sleeve and length. The useful question is not “what size do I wear?” but “which point needs room in this pattern?”

Light jackets: movement reveals the fit

A light jacket should allow a layer underneath without losing the shoulder position. Raise your arms, reach towards a shelf and sit down with the jacket closed. If the body rides up or the zip pulls diagonally, the back, bust or sleeve needs more range.

A wide cut may have more body volume and a precise sleeve. Do not measure only the total width. Check shoulder, armhole, sleeve length and where the hem ends with the inner layer. A jacket that only works when you stand still is not properly sized.

Fabric, stretch and layer thickness

Cotton jersey usually adapts more than a woven poplin shirt. Light denim keeps its body, while a twill overshirt can feel firm even when the chart shows a generous measurement. Fine knitwear may allow a closer jacket; a thick sweatshirt needs more room even if the outside volume looks similar.

Do not infer stretch from a photograph. Read the fibre content and check whether elastane is included. Also consider the thickness of the layer you will actually wear. A thin T-shirt and a sweatshirt change the decision for the same jacket.

Trousers: layers change the lower half too

If you will wear thermal underwear or tights under trousers, check the hips, thigh and rise. A waistband that closes can hide a lack of room higher on the leg. If pockets open or horizontal lines appear when you sit, the upper pattern is working too hard.

With wide-leg trousers, separate the volume of the leg from the waist and hip fit. With joggers and elastic waists, check that the waistband supports without digging in and recovers its shape. Sizing up may make the hem drag without solving a short rise.

How to decide whether to keep your size or go up

List the risk for each option. With the smaller size, which area pulls? With the larger one, which seam moves? If the smaller size limits the bust, hips or rise, sizing up may make sense. If you only need sleeve length, look for another proportion before accepting more width everywhere.

It also matters whether the garment will be worn open or closed. An open overshirt can tolerate more visual ease. A shirt under a jacket needs less outer volume. Real use belongs in the decision alongside the measurement.

Three everyday examples

For someone wearing a T-shirt and overshirt, check the overshirt's bust and back first, then the sleeve. For a child alternating between a T-shirt and fine sweatshirt, measure a sweatshirt that already allows play and raised arms instead of buying automatically by age. For a dress with a cardigan, check the dress armhole and waist and the cardigan sleeve volume.

In all three cases, the reference garment is more useful than a general rule. Bodies, patterns and fit preferences vary. The method stays the same: measure, find the fixed point and test movement.

What to look for on the product page

Words such as regular, slim, relaxed, oversize, cropped and straight are clues about design intent, not universal measurements. Check whether the garment has a dropped shoulder, raglan sleeve, elastic waist, darts, lining or closures that limit adjustment.

Reviews help when they describe something concrete: “the sleeve is short when I raise my arm”, “the back allows a T-shirt”, “the waist does not stretch” or “the fabric is firm”. A review without measurements cannot tell you to copy someone else's size, but it can tell you what to inspect.

Mistakes that add unnecessary volume

The first mistake is sizing up because of layers without deciding which layer will go underneath. The second is comparing a stretchy T-shirt with a woven jacket. The third is looking only at the front and forgetting shoulders, back, rise or sleeve length.

It is also easy to confuse an oversize silhouette with a garment that simply runs large. The volume may be deliberate and balanced by a shoulder, cuff or specific length. Sizing up changes the whole proportion.

Transitional clothing checklist

  • Define the layer you will wear underneath.
  • Compare with a garment of similar structure.
  • Measure shoulders, bust, sleeve and length on tops.
  • Check waist, hips, rise and thigh on trousers.
  • Read the fibre content, thickness and stretch.
  • Identify the cut: regular, fitted, relaxed or oversize.
  • Test movement with the garment fastened.

The movement test before removing the tags

Try the garment with the layer you will normally wear. Raise and cross your arms, sit down, turn your torso and walk a few steps. Look at the front, sides and back. Buttons should stay flat, seams should return to place and pockets should not open under tension.

If a garment only works when you stand still, do not turn it into your reference. If both sizes fail in different places, look for another cut. A size cannot always solve a proportion that does not match your body.

Turn every purchase into a useful reference

“M in jacket” is not enough. “M in twill overshirt, good at the shoulders, works over a thin T-shirt and has long sleeves” will help much more next time. Save whether you wear it open, closed or over a thicker layer.

The conclusion

Layering does not require buying one size up by default. It requires knowing what goes underneath, reading how the pattern is built and checking movement. When you prioritise shoulders, bust, waist, hips and vertical proportions, the choice stops depending on one isolated letter.

The best size is the one that keeps the garment's intention and lets you live in it. Save that reference with context and it will be worth more than an automatic conversion.
