How to Choose a Knitwear Size Online.

The label is only a starting point. Compare a garment that already fits, check the cut and decide how much room you need for the layers you usually wear.

Updated 2026Adults · Jumpers9 min read

A jumper can look perfect in a product photo but feel short in the sleeves, tight across the shoulders or far roomier than expected. With knitwear, the drape depends on the pattern and how the fabric gives: two garments in the same labelled size can feel different.

For a more informed choice, use three references together: your measurements, the dimensions of a similar garment you like, and the description of the style you want to buy. Your usual size is a starting point, not a fit guarantee.

Key takeaway

Compare centimetres and silhouette, not just XS, S or M. Measure a jumper that fits the way you want, check the product's measurement points and try it with the layer you will actually wear.

Decide how you want it to fit first

A jumper can sit close to the body, fall straight, feel roomy or be deliberately oversized. Decide what you want before opening the size chart: will you wear it over a thin T-shirt, a shirt or a thermal base layer? That choice changes how much ease feels comfortable.

Read the style description. A dropped shoulder, raglan sleeve or cropped cut behaves differently from a defined shoulder seam and straight body. Words such as regular, relaxed and oversized are useful clues, but they are not measurements shared across brands.

Measure a jumper that already fits

Choose a knit with a similar weight and drape. Lay it flat without stretching the fabric, smooth wrinkles with your hand and write down the measurements. Do not pull the cuffs or hem: knitwear can give, changing the result.

  • Shoulders: measure seam to seam when the garment has a defined shoulder seam.
  • Chest: measure pit to pit; double the width if the product page lists a circumference.
  • Length: measure from the high shoulder point to the hem, or from the neck if that is how the listing defines it.
  • Sleeve: follow the stated route; some brands measure from the shoulder, others from the centre back.

Product pages do not always measure a jumper in the same way. Check the brand's diagram or notes, and compare only figures taken between equivalent points. For a body-measurement reference, see our guide to choosing a size for transitional clothing.

How to read the numbers: an example

Imagine your reference jumper measures 54 cm pit to pit, while the new product page lists a 108 cm chest circumference. Those figures are comparable if both describe the finished garment: 54 cm laid flat is roughly 108 cm around. If the chart instead gives body circumference, do not compare 54 with that number; first check what each column measures.

If your body chest is 100 cm, a 108 cm garment would have 8 cm of positive ease there. This is only an example of the difference, not a fit rule: also compare it with a jumper you like and the described cut. Brands do not all publish garment measurements or use identical measuring points.

Separate body measurements from garment measurements

A body chart recommends a size based on chest, waist, hips or other measurements. A product table may tell you how large the finished garment is. These are not interchangeable: a jumper needs ease to fall over the body, and the amount depends on the design.

If the shop lets you compare against your own clothes, use that as an additional reference. UNIQLO explains how to use clothes you already own or body measurements alongside each product's size guide. Even so, model-specific advice should take priority over a general brand rule.

Check chest, shoulder, sleeve and length together

A wider jumper does not always solve the problem. If you size up for more chest room, the shoulder and sleeve may grow too. Look at several dimensions together and identify the part of the fit that actually concerns you.

Try it with your planned layer. Raise your arms, reach forward and cross them. The knit should not restrict movement or pull across your back; it should not bunch so much under the arms that the garment shifts either. Check that the cuff stays where you want and the hem still covers your torso as you move.

If you are between sizes, identify why before deciding. If you only want a roomier silhouette, compare the length and sleeves on the larger option. If the shoulders already drop too far in your usual size, a different cut may be better than sizing up. Our guide to choosing between two sizes can help structure that decision.

Look at the knit structure and how it recovers

Two jumpers made from the same fibre can drape differently. A fine knit often adapts and follows the silhouette more closely; ribbing may stretch as you put it on and recover some of its shape; a chunky cable adds volume and can feel more structured. These are tendencies, not guarantees: yarn, construction and the specific pattern all matter.

Notice where the volume sits. A dropped shoulder broadens the upper silhouette without necessarily meaning the chest is wider; ribbed cuffs and hems may fit more closely than the body; a high neck can feel different over a shirt. If the listing has front and side photos, use them to understand the shape, not to infer centimetres that are not provided.

When trying it on, gently stretch one area of the knit and release it: see whether it returns without looking wavy. You do not need to pull the garment while measuring; this check only helps you understand the fabric's feel. If the knit stays distorted or a seam pulls, the issue may be the pattern or construction, not a size that should automatically be increased.

The yarn affects the drape, not an automatic size rule

Cotton, wool and blends do not feel or stretch alike, and the stitch structure matters too. A fine knit may drape differently from a chunky rib. Fibre content is a clue, but it cannot tell you exactly how much a particular garment will give.

Do not buy a larger size on the assumption that the jumper will shrink. Follow the care instructions for that garment. Woolmark recommends flat-drying wool knitwear unless the care label allows another method; the weight of a wet garment on a hanger can distort it. The label and the product page are the references for that specific style.

A quick check before buying

  • Decide whether you want a close, straight or roomy fit.
  • Choose a reference jumper with similar weight and drape.
  • Measure without stretching the knit; note shoulders, chest, length and sleeve.
  • Check how the shop measures each dimension.
  • Try the layer you plan to wear and move your arms.
  • If you are between sizes, solve the part causing the issue; do not size up automatically.
  • Save the brand, style, size and fit notes for next time.

The best size is not one letter that always works; it is the one that suits this jumper's cut and your use. Save your reference garment and note which layer you tried. The next comparison will be much more specific.

Frequently asked questions

How do I know what jumper size to buy online?

Start with the brand's body-size chart, then check garment measurements if they are available. Compare them with a jumper of similar weight and cut that fits the way you want; the label alone cannot predict another style's fit.

Should I size up to wear a T-shirt underneath?

Not necessarily. Try the jumper with your usual base layer and check the chest, shoulders, sleeves and movement. Sizing up may also add length to the body and sleeves.

Which parts of a jumper should I measure?

If the product page gives garment measurements, compare shoulders, chest or pit-to-pit width, back length and sleeve. Check whether chest is listed flat or as a full circumference, and use the same measuring points on both garments.

Should I choose a size from my body or from a jumper I already own?

Use both references for different purposes: body measurements help identify the brand's size, while a jumper you like helps predict the drape. Do not directly compare your body circumference with the flat width of a garment.

Does wool always shrink in the wash?

No. It depends on the fibre, construction and care instructions. Follow the garment label rather than choosing a larger size on the assumption that it will shrink.
