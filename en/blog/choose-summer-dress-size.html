How to choose the right summer dress size for each fabric and cut.

The same size can feel roomy in a flowing dress and too tight in a shirt dress. Cut and fabric explain much of the difference.

Updated 202611 min readDresses · Fit

Picture two dresses hanging side by side. Both carry an M label. The first is viscose, falls loosely from the bust and moves with every step. The second is linen, with buttons, a shirt collar and a defined waist. On the rail they look equally simple; once tried on, one allows generous ease while the other turns every button into a small test of precision.

Your size has not changed between one garment and the next. The label summarises a pattern decision, but it does not tell you where the space has been placed. Choosing a summer dress well means finding where it should follow your body, where it needs ease and what the fabric will do after sitting, walking or wearing it for several hours.

Key idea

Before choosing a size, identify the least flexible point of the dress. On a shirt dress it may be the bust; on a fitted dress, the waist; on a straight cut, the hips. That area matters more than the letter on the label.

What a size chart cannot show

Body charts are the starting point. Bust, waist and hip measurements locate you within a brand's system, but two dresses designed for the same body measurements may feel different. One adds ease to create movement; another aims for a close silhouette. The difference lives in the finished pattern.

That is why asking whether a whole brand “runs big” or “runs small” is rarely enough. A better question is: how was this particular dress designed? Look at seams, waist position, skirt shape, fastenings and the stated fit. A side or back photograph can reveal more than a general conversion.

Read the cut before the size

A shirt dress often needs accuracy across shoulders and bust. If buttons pull or the shoulder seam sits in the wrong place, loosening the belt will not solve it. A wrap dress lets you adjust the waist, but it still needs enough overlap to stay closed as you move.

Fitted dresses depend on the pattern waist meeting your own waist. If it sits several centimetres too high or low, it may crease even when the horizontal measurement is correct. Straight and slip dresses look simple, yet the hips may be their critical point: the dress needs to skim without clinging or riding up while walking.

Shirred or smocked styles provide flexibility, but not unlimited flexibility. The elastic should expand without being stretched to its maximum at rest. If it is already working at full extension, comfort suffers and the neckline or straps may shift.

Linen needs room to do its job

Linen is cool and beautifully textured, but it usually offers less stretch than a jersey with elastane. It also creases naturally and can feel crisp at first. Allowing ease through the bust, waist or hips in a structured linen dress does not mean buying it too large; it allows the fabric to move with you.

Check the full fibre composition. A linen-viscose blend may drape more softly, while linen mixed with cotton can retain more body. Lining matters too. A roomy outer layer may hide a narrower lining that becomes the true limit around the hips or when sitting.

Practical example

Your waist matches size S but your bust matches M. In a button-front linen shirt dress, choose by the bust and shape the waist with the belt. Sizing down can leave a strained button line even if the waist looks more defined.

Viscose drapes, but it does not always stretch

Viscose is associated with flowing garments because it has a soft drape. Flowing, however, does not mean elastic. A dress can move freely through the skirt while still having a fixed waist, a side zip or a seam under the bust with very little give.

Read the care instructions before buying. How a garment feels also depends on how it is washed and dried. You do not need to predict an exact result; simply recognise that a delicate fibre deserves the manufacturer's care guidance and that choosing an already tight size is not a reliable strategy.

Cotton, jersey and stretch blends

Woven cotton can behave like crisp poplin, while cotton jersey adapts more readily to the body. Two labels that both say “cotton” do not promise the same fit. Notice whether the surface has structure, whether the fabric gives under gentle tension and whether the composition includes elastane.

With stretch blends, the correct size is not automatically the smallest one you can fasten. The fabric should recover without creating tension lines around the bust, waist or hips. If a print distorts or seams twist, the garment probably needs more room or a different pattern.

Four measurements worth checking

Measure around the fullest part of your bust, the narrowest part of your waist and the widest part of your hips. Keep the tape level and close to the body without pulling it tight. These three references let you enter a brand's chart with real information.

The fourth measurement is length, but not only total length. On dresses with a defined waist, compare the shoulder-to-waist distance. On strappy styles, check whether the straps adjust. If you have a long or short torso, a few centimetres can change the neckline, waist and skirt opening positions.

Use a dress that already works as your pattern

Your best reference may be in your wardrobe. Lay a similar dress that fits well on a flat surface. Measure across the chest, waist and hips, then check the length. Compare these figures with the new garment's measurements when the retailer provides them.

Do not compare a wrap dress with a shirt dress as though they were interchangeable. Choose a reference with similar construction. Add a note about the lived fit too: “snug at bust”, “comfortable waist”, “needs belt” or “easy to walk in”. Measurements show how much room exists; the note explains how that room feels.

What to do when you are between sizes

Start by locating the risk. If the smaller size may gape at the bust while the larger only needs its belt tightened, the larger offers a straightforward solution. If the larger moves the shoulder seam completely out of place, it may be better to keep the smaller size and look for a cut with more bust room.

With non-stretch fabrics, rigid fastenings and lined dresses, it is often sensible to prioritise your largest measurement. Adjustable and shirred styles offer more freedom to choose the silhouette you prefer. These are not fixed rules: use them to see which option has a manageable issue and which one changes the whole garment.

The five-minute movement test

When the dress arrives, do more than face the mirror. Fasten every button, raise your arms, sit down, walk, step up and rotate your torso. Check that the neckline stays in place, the skirt does not ride up and the waist lets you breathe naturally.

Look at the back and sides as well. Horizontal lines often signal tension; excess fabric may create pockets or move seams away from their intended position. One crease does not decide a return, but repeated strain every time you move deserves attention.

Checklist before buying

  • Identify the cut and its least adjustable point.
  • Check bust, waist, hips and pattern waist position.
  • Read the complete composition, not only the main fibre.
  • Look for lining, zips, buttons and adjustable straps.
  • Compare with a similarly constructed dress that fits well.
  • Name the specific risk of each size when between two.
  • Perform a movement test before removing tags.
  • Save the size plus fabric and cut notes in SIZES.

Common online dress-shopping mistakes

The first is choosing from the photograph without reading the stated fit. The second is assuming a waist belt can solve any lack of room at the bust or shoulders. The third is ignoring the lining. The fourth is comparing the size with a dress built in a completely different way.

Another common mistake is sizing up to solve one point and moving the whole garment out of place. If neither option fits shoulders, bust and waist reasonably well, the problem is not your body or the chart: that particular pattern may simply not suit the fit or use you need.

Turn one purchase into a useful reference

When you find a dress that works, save more than the size. Record the brand, cut, composition and the area that decided the fit. “M in linen shirt dress; good at bust with belt” is far more useful than “I wear M in this brand”.
