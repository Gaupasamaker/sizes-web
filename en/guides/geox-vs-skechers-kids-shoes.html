Geox vs Skechers Kids: how to compare school shoe sizes.

Two pairs can show similar numbers and still feel very different. Measure both feet, check length, width, fastening and use before deciding.

Updated 202611 min readKids · School
Our advice

Do not turn a Geox size into a Skechers size without checking the model. Start by measuring both feet while standing, use the longer one, compare the official chart and test that the shoe supports movement without blocking it.

What the official guides say

The official Geox size guide explains how to measure a foot and use that reference with its girl, boy and baby charts. It is the starting point for the actual purchase, not a universal conversion for every footwear family.

The Skechers Spain school shoe guide brings together school options and shows that fastening can vary between laces, elastic, Velcro and other systems. Check the current product page because the fit depends on that construction.

Official sources matter because charts and collections change. Save the link or measurement you used, then check again if the model, country, unit or season changes. A general guide cannot replace the product information for the pair you are about to order.

The school year starts with the foot, not the label

A school shoe may be worn for hours: the walk to school, the classroom, break time, stairs and sometimes an after-school activity. A pair that seems fine while sitting may start to irritate when the child walks, runs or carries a backpack. The shoe number is only a translation of a measurement, not a permanent identity.

Feet also change during the day. Under body weight they spread slightly, and the sock takes up space. Measuring just after waking and trying the shoes in the afternoon with a thicker sock means comparing two different situations. Your method should resemble the real school day.

Before looking at Geox or Skechers, define the job the shoes must do. Do they need to withstand a daily uniform? Does the child need a fastening they can manage independently? Will they also be used for PE? The answer lets you compare like with like and read the differences more accurately.

How to measure both feet at home

Place a sheet of paper against a wall and ask the child to put their heel against it without curling their toes. They should stand with weight balanced and wear the school sock they will actually use. Mark the furthest point of the longest toe, then repeat the process for the other foot.

Measure from the edge of the paper to each mark in millimetres and write down both numbers. Do not round too early or use an average: the longer foot needs the room. If a result looks strange, repeat the test and check that the heel stayed against the wall.

A traced outline can be a useful visual check, but it is not the exact internal length of a shoe. The insole, toe box, sole, materials and shape affect the final space. The measurement gets you into the chart; movement confirms whether the whole shoe works.

Length and room in front of the toes

A child needs to move their toes and have functional room in front, but too much space allows the foot to slide. The right amount depends on the shoe structure, flexibility, fastening and use. It cannot be decided from the European size alone.

Check that the toes do not touch the front while the child is standing and that the heel stays secure when walking. If the foot moves forward, the issue may be width or fastening rather than length. If the toes touch or curl, there is not enough room even if the number looks familiar.

If the design allows the insole to be removed, let the child stand on it with the heel in the correct position. It is a clue, not a scientific measurement, and should be read with the child's feedback. On a fixed insole, use toe freedom, heel hold and movement together.

Width, instep and the last

Length gets most of the attention, but many problems appear at the sides or across the instep. Look for pressure marks, crowded toes, difficulty getting the foot in or seams that are pulling. Ask where the child feels the problem and do not translate “tight” automatically into “I need a longer size”.

A structured school shoe can distribute space differently from a mesh trainer. The toe box may be firmer, the tongue thicker or the heel more built up. With Skechers, pay close attention to the volume and fastening of the chosen family; with Geox, study the construction and the space provided by that particular model.

If length works but the side is compressing, look for another last, fastening or volume. A longer shoe does not correct a high instep or a wide forefoot. It may make the heel move and cause the child to drag their feet to keep the pair on.

Velcro, laces and independence

Fastening is part of sizing because it determines how the foot is held. Two pairs with the same length can feel different when one has two Velcro straps and the other has laces with a more padded tongue. The child should be able to close the shoe with enough tension, without leaving it loose or over-tightening it.

For school, independence matters as much as material. Ask the child to sit, put the shoe on, adjust the Velcro or tie the lace and stand up without help. Watch whether the heel stays, whether the tongue shifts and whether the closure sits in a place that does not rub while walking.

Elastic can make a shoe easy to use, but it does not replace support. A very easy fastening may still be too loose if the model does not match the instep. Try the real sock and both feet because volume can change the experience.

What can change between model families

Geox and Skechers are not single shoe shapes. Each brand includes school shoes, trainers, boots, slip-ons and designs with different soles and insoles. A good experience with one pair does not automatically turn any other pair into the same size decision.

A black Velcro school shoe may have a more defined volume and a firmer heel than a light mesh trainer. A flexible trainer may bend earlier and give a different sense of width. A thicker sole may lift the foot inside the instep. These product differences belong in the comparison alongside the brand.

When comparing, choose two models with the same purpose. Do not compare a structured Geox school shoe with a casual Skechers trainer and blame every difference on the number. Match the use first, then compare measurement, fastening, last and behaviour.

Classroom, playground and after-school activities

For a long school day, look at heel stability, sole flex and how easily the foot can be adjusted. A child can walk a lot without doing organised sport. The shoe should follow the step and let the toes move without letting the foot slide forward.

At break time, durability and fastening become important. Check that Velcro stays closed while running and that laces do not come loose. If the pair will also be used for PE, compare it with an equivalent sports model rather than with a dress shoe simply because both are black.

Clothing changes the use as well. The school uniform may come with thin socks, while an after-school activity uses thicker sports socks. Record that difference and do not expect one size decision to cover situations that share neither sock nor movement.

An example between two sizes

Imagine Paula measures 218 millimetres on the left foot and 221 on the right. A Geox model holds her heel well, but the toe box feels firm on the longer foot. A flexible Skechers model has enough length, but the heel lifts while walking because its fastening does not adjust in the same way.

Moving up a Geox size might solve the toe pressure, but it could also create too much space behind. Choosing a longer Skechers size would not fix a loose fastening. The useful decision requires another look at the model: perhaps the first case needs a wider shape and the second a different closure.

The example is not a universal conversion. It shows how to separate the questions: is length missing, is there too much volume, is the width tight, is the heel moving, can the child adjust the shoe? Once you identify the variable, the comparison stops depending on instinct.

SIZES tip: keep a full note: “longer foot 221 mm, school sock, Geox, two Velcro straps, firm toe box, stable heel”. A measurement without context cannot tell you how the shoe worked during the school day.

What to do between two sizes

Start by identifying which measurement separates the two options. If the smaller size is short, the decision may be clear. If length works but the side is tight, sizing up can add room in front without solving the pressure. If the heel moves in both, change the last or fastening.

Reasonable growth matters, but it should not turn the shoe into a solution for three months from now. The first school weeks need immediate comfort and stability for walking, running and stairs. Try the larger option with the real sock and watch whether the foot slides or the child loses confidence.

If one measurement is on the limit, review the insole, instep volume and toe-box shape too. Sometimes a different model within the same brand solves the problem better than changing the number. Save why you chose the size so the next purchase starts with an actual experience.

How to compare Geox and Skechers without inventing an equivalence

Open both official charts and locate the longer foot's length in the unit each brand uses. Write down the resulting size, but also record the model type, fastening, insole and any width information. If a page gives product-specific advice, give it priority over a general table.

Then compare the product pages with the same question: how will this shoe hold this child's foot? It is not enough to say that a brand “runs large” or “runs small”. That sentence may describe one model, one sock or one fit preference and may not apply to the next pair.

Finally, define an exit plan. When shopping online, check the return period and conditions before removing tags. Try the shoes indoors on a clean surface with the real sock. A responsible comparison includes what you will do if the heel moves, toes touch or the child cannot close the shoe.

Common school-shoe mistakes

The first is copying last year's size without measuring again. The second is measuring only one foot. The third is buying much too large because of growth. It is also common to compare a trainer with a school shoe and blame the brand for a difference that belongs to the pattern.

Another mistake is checking length alone. A foot can have room in front but be compressed at the side, or have the correct width but lose stability because the heel is not held. The fastening, insole, sock and material are all part of the sizing experience.

Do not turn another child's experience into a rule either. Save your child's measurement, model and use. When their feet grow, you will know what part of the decision can be repeated and what needs a fresh check.

The five-minute movement test

With the usual sock, let the child walk, stop, turn, make a gentle jump and climb one step. Ask them to crouch and carry a backpack if the shoes are for school. Watch whether the heel stays put and whether the sole bends naturally.

Ask where they feel pressure: front, side, instep or heel. Check that they can put the pair on and remove it, and that the fastening does not leave deep marks. The child's answer is valuable, but it should sit alongside what you see in their posture and movement.

Our final recommendation

Geox and Skechers may offer different solutions for a school day, but no label replaces measuring the foot. Use the longer foot as your reference, check the current chart, review width and fastening, and test the pair through real movement.

The best size lets a child walk, sit, run and get dressed without constantly thinking about the shoe. When you save the measurement and model, the next purchase starts with a useful clue instead of a guess.

Frequently asked questions

Do Geox and Skechers have the same kids' size?

Do not assume it. Equivalence depends on foot measurement, the chart and the exact model, as well as width, fastening and use.

Should I measure both feet before buying?

Yes. Measure both while standing in the usual sock and use the longer one as the reference. Repeat the measurement if the sock or activity changes.

Should I size up for growth?

Only if length and the movement test support it. Too much room in front can reduce stability and will not solve a width, volume or fastening problem.

What if the shoe is tight even though the length fits?

Review the last, toe box, instep and fastening. Look for another model or width before choosing a longer size that may make the heel move.
